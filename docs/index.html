The Great Decoupling: Detecting Silent Click Loss in Search Content

Ridoy Hasan · FlyRank ML Engineering Internship · Capstone, 2026

Abstract. FlyRank's content strategists manage search performance for dozens of client portfolios, and face a real, growing blind spot: since AI Overviews became standard in Google Search, a page can hold steady impressions and ranking while its actual clicks quietly disappear — a pattern with no name or detection method in FlyRank's existing tooling. This project asks whether that pattern, which industry research calls the "Great Decoupling," can be identified early enough from observable search signals for a strategist to act on. Using 123,683 real content pages from FlyRank's 79-million-row production search warehouse, we built a transparent baseline rule and a Random Forest classifier trained only on February 2026 signals, validated against March 2026 outcomes on a client-grouped holdout split. The baseline rule failed to beat random selection on this split (precision@50 of 0.00), while the Random Forest achieved a 2.58x lift over the base rate (precision@50 of 0.08, ROC AUC 0.81) — evidence the added complexity earned its place. The result is a ranked, human-reviewed action playbook intended to help FlyRank's content strategists triage which client pages need review first, before the click loss becomes visible in standard reporting.

Introduction

FlyRank manages content performance across dozens of client sites, and its strategists rely on standard dashboards — impressions, average position, sessions — to decide where to spend limited review time each week. That approach has a specific, current weakness: published industry research (Ahrefs, Pew Research, 2026) shows AI Overviews correlate with meaningfully lower click-through rates even when a page's impressions and ranking positions hold steady — a page can look completely healthy on the metrics FlyRank's team already watches, while its actual value to the client quietly erodes. This is not a hypothetical concern raised for this project; it's the real motivation this lane was chosen for in Week 1 of this internship, after confirming the pattern already appears in FlyRank's own starter data. This paper asks a narrow, concrete version of that problem: given only what was knowable about a page in a given month, can a model flag it as being at risk of this pattern before a strategist would otherwise notice — and does that model actually outperform a simple, transparent rule a human could apply by hand?

Data

Release: FlyRank/internship-warehouse (Hugging Face, gated), build flyrank_pseudonymized_warehouse_release_v20260703.

Tables used: fact_content_daily_performance (February and March 2026 partitions) and dim_content (content_type, main_intent, content_updated_date — static metadata only).

Date windows: February 1–28, 2026 (features) and March 1–31, 2026 (outcome). Both mid-panel months — never the sealed final month (June 2026), which is reserved by the program as a held-out test month.

Deliberately excluded:

  • fact_content_query_90d — its window overlaps recent months, a documented leakage risk.
  • GA4 columns — not needed for a GSC-only signal.
  • Any FlyRank product decision flags (health_score, priority_score, etc.) — not shipped in this dataset by design.

Public-safety: all identifiers are pseudonymized hashes; no raw client names, domains, or search queries appear anywhere in this analysis.

Methodology

Label: decoupling_signature = 1 if impressions changed between −10% and +10% (Feb→March) AND clicks dropped 15% or more over the same comparison. A defined-rule proxy, not an observed business outcome — disclosed as such throughout this project.

Features (February/prior-window only, nothing from March): impressions_prior30, clicks_prior30, ctr_prior30, avg_position_prior30, content_type, main_intent.

Baseline: a transparent, hand-written rule — score = visible × ctr_gap_prior30, where ctr_gap_prior30 compares each page's CTR to the median CTR for its own position bucket. No fitted weights.

Model: Random Forest Classifier (300 trees), chosen after Logistic Regression as a readable first pass — picked because the question is a yes/no observed label, with complexity added only after it earned its place.

Validation design: GroupShuffleSplit grouped by client_hash_id (25% test), so no client appears in both train and test — protects against the model memorizing client-specific patterns rather than learning something generalizable.

Leakage checks performed: (1) a feature-name audit against outcome-window and label terms; (2) a deliberate re-add of a label-derived column to confirm the audit harness actually detects leakage when present; (3) a random-vs-grouped split comparison to check for a client-memorization gap; (4) a candidate freshness feature (content_updated_date) was tested and dropped after discovering its values fell mostly after the decision point (median −50 days relative to the window close) — a genuine future-leakage risk caught before it reached the model, not assumed away.

Results

Evaluated on an identical held-out test split (25% of clients, grouped so no client appears in both train and test), same metric, same label.

Base rate (test set): 3.11%.

The transparent baseline rule caught none of its top 50 flagged pages on this held-out split — performing at or below chance here. The Random Forest meaningfully outperformed both the baseline and random selection, correctly prioritizing roughly 4 of its top 50 picks versus the ~1.6 expected by chance at this base rate. This is the clearest evidence in this project that the added model complexity earned its place, on this lane's data.

Limitations & Honest Framing

  • The label is a defined-rule proxy (Feb-vs-March comparison), not a confirmed AI-Overview cause — this ranks a pattern, it does not diagnose why it happened.
  • Single lane: GSC signals only. No content-quality, backlink, or competitor signals.
  • content_updated_date could not be used as a leak-free freshness feature: values ranged from −97 to 303 days relative to the March 31 decision point (median −50), meaning most recorded update dates fall after the observation window. This feature was dropped from the ranking rather than reported as a finding.
  • Evaluated on one client-grouped split, one month pair (Feb→March 2026) — not yet tested against a different season or a newly onboarded client.

The "High-risk" tier (top 20% by model score) is the intended review queue — roughly 27,000 pages flagged out of 123,683, still far too many to review at once. In practice, a strategist would combine this with the model's raw score to review the top N they have capacity for (e.g. the top 50–200), not the full high-risk tier at once. None of this should be automated: no auto-publishing, no auto-redirects, and no using this queue to evaluate an individual writer's performance, since the label reflects portfolio-wide search dynamics outside any one person's control.
